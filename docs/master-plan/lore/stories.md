# Stories with wisdom

Short original stories for reading aloud at bedtime, in class, or inside a game as a loading story or a chapter opening. They use the cast of the [character bible](characters.md), the home world [Raya](raya.md), the rusted world [Shacados](shacados.md) and the meeting of the two in [First Contact](first-contact.md). Many of them happen in the places of the [scenery pilots](scenery-pilots.md), so a story and a picture can go together.

Every story ends with one line from the [wisdom bank](README.md#wisdom-bank) or another line of canon, and with two "Talk about it" questions for a parent or teacher. The stories follow the [tone of voice](README.md#tone-of-voice) and pass the [adorability gate](adorability-gate.md).

Status: **draft for maintainer review.** Story names and small details that the lore does not already state are _(proposal)_. The only new named characters are Sora _(proposal)_, who also appears in the scenery pilots, and Pell _(proposal)_, a child of the Last Woods.

**Age rules for these stories.** The tiers follow the [age tiers](characters.md#age-tiers); the children's tier is split into 6–9 and 10–12 by reading level.

- **1–3 and 3–5:** only Link creatures, Orbis, Raya's meadows and gentle Rayan children. No Shacados, no danger, no death, no villains.
- **6–9:** Raya's everyday life: the Guard, Renewal, the towers. Problems are puzzles and misunderstandings.
- **10–12:** Shacados may appear through repair, sharing and hope. Hard things are named, never shown.
- **13+:** honest about risk, poverty and fear, but never hopeless. Violence stays off the page, and every story has someone who shares, mends, teaches or refuses to harm.

## For the littlest (1–3, read aloud)

### Hello, Hello, Let's Link!

_Ages 1–3 · Omi, a sunny meadow · Linky, Soru & Tari, Itsy, Nully, Anya_

Here is Linky.
Linky has one red eye.
Linky has one blue eye.
Linky has two hands.

Linky walks in the meadow.
Hop, hop, hop.

Who is that? It is Soru and Tari.
Soru is red. Tari is blue.
"Hello, Soru! Hello, Tari!" says Linky.
"Let's link!"
Linky holds out two hands.
Soru takes one hand. Tari takes one hand.
Now they are three.
Hop, hop, hop.

Who is that? It is Itsy.
Itsy is small and round.
Itsy is holding Itsy's own hand.
"Hello, Itsy!" says Linky.
"Let's link!"
Itsy smiles. Itsy takes Tari's hand.
Now they are four.
Hop, hop, hop.

Who is that? Shh. It is Nully.
Nully is sleepy.
Nully is sitting on a soft round seat.
"Hello, Nully," whispers Linky.
"Let's link?"
Nully yawns a little yawn.
Nully takes Itsy's hand, very gently.
Now they are five.
Tip, tip, tip. Quiet steps for Nully.

Who is that? It is a twinkly star.
The star has a question mark on its tummy.
It is Anya!
"Can I come too?" asks Anya.
"Yes!" says Linky.
"Yes!" say Soru and Tari.
"Yes!" says Itsy.
"Yes," yawns Nully.
Anya takes Nully's hand.
Now they are six.

Six friends in a line.
Red and blue. Small and round.
Sleepy and twinkly.
And Linky, at the front.

They walk in a big, big circle.
Hop, hop, hop.
Tip, tip, tip.
Around the meadow they go.
Around and around.

The circle comes back to the start.
Anya is next to Linky now.
Linky holds out a hand.
Anya takes it.
The circle is closed.
No one is at the end.
Everyone is in the middle of the friends.

"Hello, friends," says Linky.
"Hello, Linky!" say all the friends.
And they all sit down in the grass,
holding hands,
in one big round circle.

> **Wisdom:** "Everyone is welcome." — Anya

Talk about it:

1. Who did Linky meet first? Who came last? Can you count all the friends on your fingers?
2. Linky asked everyone, "Let's link?" Who would you like to hold hands with today?

### Nully's Nap

_Ages 1–3 · Omi, Postie's house by the pond · Nully, Linky, Verbi, Oopsy_

This is Nully.
Nully is soft.
Nully is see-through, like a bubble.
Nully sits on seat number zero.

Nully is sleepy.
Nully closes Nully's eyes.
Nap time.

Here comes Verbi.
Verbi likes to jump.
"Jump! Jump!" says Verbi.
Boing, boing, boing!
Then Verbi sees Nully.
Nully is napping.
"Oh," says Verbi.
"Shh."
Verbi does a tiny jump.
Boing.
A very tiny boing.

Here comes Oopsy.
Oopsy has a band-aid and a big smile.
Oopsy is carrying a drum.
"Drum! Drum!" says Oopsy.
Bom, bom, bom!
Then Oopsy sees Nully.
Nully is napping.
"Oops," says Oopsy.
"Shh."
Oopsy taps the drum with one finger.
Pat.
A very tiny pat.

Here comes Linky.
Linky likes to sing.
"La, la, la!" sings Linky.
Then Linky sees Nully.
Nully is napping.
"Oh," says Linky.
"Shh."
Linky sings very, very softly.
La... la... la...
It sounds like a lullaby.

Now everyone is quiet.
Verbi sits down.
Oopsy sits down.
Linky sits down.
The wind is quiet.
The pond is quiet.
Even the frogs are quiet.

What are they doing?
Nothing.
They are doing nothing.
Is nothing something?
Yes. Nothing is resting.

Verbi's eyes get heavy.
Oopsy's eyes get heavy.
Linky's eyes get heavy.
Yaaawn.
Everyone is napping now.
Shh.

The sun moves a little.
A dragonfly lands on the drum.
Then Nully opens Nully's eyes.
Nully looks around.
Everyone is asleep!
Nully smiles a soft, round smile.

Nully does not wake them.
Nully sits very still.
Nully waits.
Nully is very good at waiting.

One by one, the friends wake up.
Verbi stretches. Boing!
Oopsy stretches. Oops!
Linky stretches. "Hello, Nully!"
"Hello," says Nully.
"Thank you for the quiet."

Then Verbi jumps.
Oopsy drums.
Linky sings.
And Nully?
Nully claps along.
Clap, clap, clap.
Now it is play time.

> **Wisdom:** "Rest is important. Nothing is also something." — Nully

Talk about it:

1. What did Verbi, Oopsy and Linky do when they saw Nully napping?
2. When do you feel sleepy? What do you like to do when you rest?

### Pip and the Dew Drop

_Ages 1–3 · Raya, the clover meadow by a short white tower · Pip the Orbi, a fawn_

This is Pip.
Pip is round and white.
Pip floats in the air.
Pip has one red eye.
Pip has one blue eye.
Boop-bip! That is how Pip says hello.

This is the meadow.
The grass is green.
The clover is soft.
Far away is a tall white tower.
It is thin, like a flower stem.

Pip floats over the clover.
Look! A dew drop.
The dew drop sits on a leaf.
It is round. It is shiny.
It is like a tiny Pip.

Pip asks the leaf, "May I?"
The leaf nods in the wind.
Pip lifts the dew drop up.
Up, up, up.
The dew drop floats in the air.
It does not fall.
Pip is holding it, very gently.
Inside the drop is a tiny rainbow.

Who is that?
It is a fawn.
The fawn has spots.
The fawn has big dark eyes.
The fawn steps out of the tall grass.
Step. Step. Step.

The fawn looks at the dew drop.
The fawn sniffs.
Sniff, sniff.
Pip's eyes smile.
Boop-bip!

Pip holds the drop near the fawn.
Not too close. Not too fast.
The fawn sniffs again.
Sniff, sniff.

Then Pip lets go.
Gently, gently.
The drop falls.
Plip!
It lands on the fawn's nose.

The fawn jumps!
Hop!
The fawn shakes its head.
The fawn licks its nose.
The fawn is not scared.
The fawn wants another one!

Pip lifts another drop.
Plip!
And another.
Plip, plop!
The fawn hops and hops.

Now the sun is high.
The fawn is tired.
The fawn lies down in the clover.
Pip is tired too.
Pip makes its light soft and low.
Pip floats down beside the fawn.

Two friends in the clover.
One with spots.
One round and white.
The wind sings in the grass.
The tower stands far away.
Everything is quiet.

Goodnight, fawn.
Goodnight, Pip.
Plip.

> **Wisdom:** "Let go gently." — Letgo

Talk about it:

1. What did Pip do with the dew drop? Can you be a fawn and say "Plip!"?
2. Pip asked "May I?" before taking the drop. When do you ask "May I?"

## For preschoolers (3–5)

### Oopsy's Wobbly Tower

_Ages 3–5 · Omi, Linky's garden · Oopsy, Verbi, Linky, Akoren_

Oopsy wanted to build the tallest tower in all of Omi.

Oopsy is a little robot with a band-aid on one arm and a big smile on its face. It had a basket full of blocks: red ones, blue ones and one green one.

"Build!" shouted Verbi, jumping up and down. "Build, build, build!"

Oopsy put down a red block. Then a blue block. Then a red block. Then a blue block. The tower grew and grew. It was taller than Verbi. It was taller than Linky. It wobbled a little.

"One more!" said Oopsy, and put the green block on the very top.

Wobble. Wobble. Wobble.

Crash! The blocks tumbled all over the grass.

Oopsy sat down. Its smile went small. "Oops," it said quietly.

Linky sat down next to Oopsy. "That was a really big crash," said Linky. "Did you hear it go wobble first?"

Oopsy nodded. "Wobble, wobble, wobble."

"I wonder why it wobbled," said Linky.

Akoren, the old tree, rustled his leaves. "Look at my roots," he said in his slow, deep voice. "What do you see?"

Oopsy looked. Akoren's roots spread out wide, wide, wide, under the grass.

"Your bottom is bigger than your top!" said Oopsy.

"Yes," said Akoren. "That is how I stand up in the wind."

Oopsy's smile came back, big again. "Oops means I'm learning!" it said. "Let's try again!"

This time, Oopsy put four blocks on the bottom, side by side. Then three blocks on top of those. Then two. Then one.

"Build!" shouted Verbi.

Then Oopsy put more blocks on top, one at a time. The tower grew and grew. It was taller than Verbi. It was taller than Linky. It was almost as tall as Akoren's first branch.

Oopsy held the green block. Everyone held their breath.

Oopsy put it on the very top.

No wobble.

"Hooray!" shouted Verbi, and jumped so high that he landed in Akoren's leaves.

Linky clapped. Oopsy spun around three times. Then it bumped into the tower, just a little.

Wobble.

Everyone looked at the tower. The tower stood still.

"Oops," said Oopsy, and this time everyone laughed, Oopsy most of all.

> **Wisdom:** "Oops means you're learning." — Oopsy

Talk about it:

1. Why did the first tower fall down? What did Oopsy change the second time?
2. Tell about a time you said "oops". What did you learn?

### Postie and the Letter with No Name

_Ages 3–5 · Omi, the pond and the hill · Postie Index, Itsy, Linky, Nully_

Postie Index is a snail. Postie carries a satchel full of envelopes, and every envelope has a number on it. The number tells Postie where to go.

Number 1 is Linky's house. Number 2 is the twins' house. Number 0 is Nully's soft seat. Postie knows them all.

One morning, Postie found a letter at the bottom of the satchel. It had no number on it. It had no name. It just had a drawing of a small circle.

"Oh dear," said Postie slowly, because snails say everything slowly. "Who is this for?"

Postie went to Linky's house. Linky looked at the circle. "It is round," said Linky. "But it is not me. I am a link, with two ends."

Postie went to the pond. The twins were splashing. "It is not for us," said Soru and Tari. "We are two. That is only one circle."

Postie went to Nully's seat. Nully was waking up from a nap. Nully looked at the drawing for a long time.

"It looks like me," said Nully softly. "But my circle is empty. This circle has a little arrow going around it, back to where it started."

Postie thought. Postie thought slowly, the way snails do. A circle with an arrow that comes back to itself. Who goes back to themself?

"Itsy!" said Postie.

Itsy lives at the top of the hill. Itsy is small and round, and Itsy always holds Itsy's own hand. Itsy is a link from itself to itself.

It is a long way up the hill for a snail. Postie went up, up, up, one slow step at a time. Linky walked beside Postie. Nully floated behind.

At the top, Itsy was sitting alone, holding Itsy's own hand.

"A letter for you," said Postie.

Itsy opened it. Inside was a picture of all the friends, holding hands in a circle. Under it, someone had drawn a small round Itsy, right in the middle.

"Who sent it?" asked Itsy.

Postie smiled. "Anya did. She wanted you to know there is a place for you."

Itsy laughed and let go of Itsy's own hand, just for a moment, to hold Linky's.

"Now," said Postie, taking out a pencil, "let's write your number on your door. Then no letter will ever get lost again."

> **Wisdom:** "No one is lost; everyone has an address." — Postie

Talk about it:

1. How did Postie find out who the letter was for?
2. Do you know your address? Why is it good to have one?

### The Apple Can Wait

_Ages 3–5 · Raya, the old apple tree at dawn · Mira, Pip the Orbi_

Mira woke up in her nest, high in a big blue-green tree. Her Orbi, Pip, floated over with two sleepy eyes, one red and one blue.

"Good morning, Pip," said Mira.

"Good morning, Mira," said Pip.

They climbed down the soft ladder of her nest together, slowly, through the leaves and the mist, to the meadow below. Pip floated beside her all the way. The grass was wet and cold on Mira's toes.

At the edge of the forest stood the old apple tree. One apple hung low. It was round and red and shiny.

Mira's tummy rumbled. She reached up.

"Ask first," said Pip.

Mira stopped. "Ask who? It's a tree."

"Ask the tree," said Pip. "Look closely. Is anyone living there?"

Mira looked. She looked at the trunk. She looked at the branches. And there, on the branch right next to the apple, was a tiny nest woven from grass. Inside were three blue eggs.

"Oh," whispered Mira. "If I pull the apple, the branch will shake."

"And the eggs will shake," said Pip.

Mira put her hand down. "But I'm hungry," she said.

"I can help," said Pip. Pip floated up to the apple, very slowly, very quietly. A thin ring of light came out of Pip and passed through the apple, from top to bottom, like a soft hoop. The apple did not move. Not one leaf fell. The eggs did not shake at all.

"I know this apple now," said Pip. "Every bit of it. Tonight, the garden in your nest will make one just like it."

"Tomorrow?" asked Mira.

"Tomorrow," said Pip. "One night for one apple."

Mira ate yesterday's copied berries for breakfast instead.

That night, when the two moons, Pim and Pom, came up over the trees, the garden in Mira's nest hummed a quiet hum. Slowly, slowly, an apple grew in a little cradle of white.

In the morning, Mira took a big crunchy bite. It was sweet and juicy, exactly like the real one.

She climbed down and ran to the old tree to look. The apple was still there, round and red. A small bird sat on the branch, pecking at it happily. And in the nest, one blue egg had a crack in it.

"Good morning, bird," said Mira, very softly.

> **Wisdom:** "Copy, don't take." — Orbi

Talk about it:

1. Why did Mira not pick the apple? What did she find on the branch?
2. Is there something you like that you could share or leave for someone else?

## For young readers (6–9)

### Theo Asks First

_Ages 6–9 · Raya, a river beach by a tower · Theo, Mira, their Orbis_

Theo had a plan, and it was a very good plan.

The class had landed on the pebble beach after their afternoon ride along the river. Everybody's legs were wobbly from flying. Mira was standing on a flat rock at the edge of the water, watching the silver fish flick back and forth.

Theo crept up behind her. One step. Two steps. He held out his hands. He was going to give her the tiniest push, and she would go splash, and everybody would laugh.

He pushed.

His arms stopped.

They stopped in the air, as if he had pushed into a big soft pillow that nobody could see. He wobbled, waved his arms, and sat down on the pebbles with a bump.

Mira turned around. She looked at Theo sitting on the stones with his hands still up in the air. Then she started to laugh. And then Theo started to laugh too, even though his ears had gone bright red.

"The Guard," said his Orbi calmly. "The rock is slippery, and the water is shallow there. Mira could have hit her head."

Theo knew about the Guard. Everybody did. It never told you what to think. It never told you off. It just paused your arm or your leg for a moment when a move might hurt someone. Then it let go.

"I was only joking," said Theo.

"I know," said his Orbi. "The Guard does not read your thoughts. Thoughts are free. It only watched your hands."

A thin thread of light appeared between Mira's Orbi and Theo's Orbi. Mira was speaking to him through the Chord, with her mouth closed and her eyes sparkling.

"Next time ask," said her voice inside his head, "and I'll push you back."

Theo stood up and brushed off his trousers. He walked over to Mira's rock, but this time he stopped in front of her.

"Mira," he said out loud. "May I push you into the river?"

Mira thought about it. Then she pointed. "Not here. Over there, into the deep green pool. Then it's fun, and nobody gets hurt."

They walked to the deep pool together. Theo pushed. His arms did not stop.

Splash!

Mira came up spluttering and laughing. Then she grabbed Theo's ankle.

"Your turn," she said. "Yes?"

"Yes!"

Splash!

> **Wisdom:** "Ask first." — Orbi

Talk about it:

1. Why did the Guard stop Theo the first time, but not the second time?
2. What games do you play where it is important to ask first?

### The Professor's Hands

_Ages 6–9 · Raya, a reading nest at dusk · Trip, Doubl, Professor Acorn_

Professor Acorn was the only old person Trip had ever seen.

On Raya, the grown-ups all looked young. Trip's mother was three hundred years old, and she looked like a big sister. His teacher was more than a thousand, and she could still do cartwheels on a sky-lane. Grown-ups chose to stay young, and almost all of them did.

But Professor Acorn had white hair, and round spectacles shaped like a sideways eight, and hands that looked like the bark of a tree.

One evening, the children sat on cushions in the reading nest high in the treetops. The towers outside were glowing softly. The Professor was reading a story about a snail. When he turned the page, Trip reached out and touched the back of his hand.

"Why is your skin like a dry leaf?" Trip asked.

Doubl gasped. "Trip! You can't ask that!"

But the Professor laughed his slow, warm laugh. "Of course he can. It is a very good question. Questions are seeds."

He took off his spectacles and wiped them on the sleeve of his white suit, the one with the leaf pattern.

"A long, long time ago," he said, "I looked just like your mother. Young and quick. Then one day I wondered: if nobody ever grows old, how will children know what old looks like? How will they know what it feels like to change all the way, like a tree changes from spring to autumn?"

"So you switched it off?" asked Doubl.

"So I switched it off," said the Professor. "So that you would know. The healers' Orbis still help me when I am sick. But my face, my hair and my hands are free to change now."

His Orbi floated closer and showed a picture in the air: a young man with dark hair and the very same spectacles.

Trip looked at the picture. He looked at the Professor. He looked at the picture again.

"It's the same you," he said.

"It is the same me," said the Professor. "A tree with many rings is still the same tree."

"Does it hurt?" asked Trip.

"Sometimes my knees grumble on the stairs," said the Professor. "And sometimes it is wonderful. I can feel time going by, like a river around my feet."

From a pocket of his suit he took a small brown acorn and put it in Trip's hand.

"Plant it tomorrow," he said. "Someday it will have bark like my hands. And it will still be the same little acorn."

> **Wisdom:** "Big trees grow from tiny acorns." — Akoren

Talk about it:

1. How is Professor Acorn different from the other grown-ups on Raya? Why did he choose it?
2. Look at a photo of someone in your family when they were young. What has changed, and what is still the same?

### The Tower That Sang Late

_Ages 6–9 · Raya, the tower festival on a flower steppe · Doubl, Trip, Lina_

Once a year, the towers of Raya sing out loud.

The rest of the time they hum in sounds too high for people to hear, sending messages and energy along the Chord. But on the night of the festival, after the baby birds have learned to fly, they play music that everyone can hear.

Doubl and Trip sat in the long grass of the flower steppe with Lina, waiting. The near tower stood tall and white beside them. The far tower was just a tiny white line on the edge of the world.

Boooommm.

The near tower played one deep, low note. Trip felt it in his tummy. The grass trembled.

Then nothing.

People waited, smiling. Children started to fidget. Doubl counted. "Ten... twenty... fifty... eighty..."

"The far tower forgot!" said Trip.

Boooommm.

The answer rolled across the steppe, softer, from far away. It fit together with the first note like two hands holding each other.

"It's late!" said Trip. "It was ninety seconds late!"

"Was it?" asked Lina, and her eyes twinkled. "What would the people who live near the far tower say?"

Doubl thought. "For them, the far tower is near," she said slowly. "They hear their own tower first."

"And then?" asked Lina.

"And then they hear our tower, late!" said Trip.

Lina nodded. "Sound needs time to travel. Here, it takes about three seconds to go one kilometer. The far tower is thirty kilometers away. So every note needs about ninety seconds to get here."

"So nobody was late," said Doubl. "It's just where you stand."

"Exactly," said Lina. "And the people who wrote the song knew that. They made the music as a round. Each tower's answer arrives exactly when the melody needs it, wherever you are listening."

Boooommm, went the near tower. And far away, at that same moment, the people by the far tower heard their own tower sing, and waited for ours.

Trip lay back in the grass and listened to the slow, enormous song going back and forth across the steppe, like two friends calling to each other across a wide valley.

"Lina," he said sleepily, "do the stars sing late too?"

"Light is much, much faster than sound," said Lina. "But the stars are very far away. So yes. A little."

> **Wisdom:** "Truth can depend on where you stand." — Rela

Talk about it:

1. Why did Trip think the far tower was late? Was he right?
2. Next time you see lightning, count the seconds until the thunder. What does the number tell you?

## For older readers (10–12)

### Three Dead Radios

_Ages 10–12 · Shacados, a mender's stall under a railway bridge · Teyo, Old Brann_

Rain dripped from the rivets of the railway bridge, one cold drop at a time. Under it, in a stall made of crates and old canvas, Old Brann was opening radios.

Teyo had found all three of them in the scrap dunes. One had a cracked case. One had no speaker. One was so full of dust that it had probably not made a sound in twenty years. He had carried them back on his shoulders, hoping Brann could pay a little for the parts.

Brann did not pay. Instead he said, "Sit."

His hands were wide and strong, with shiny scars across the backs from years of welding. They moved slowly, but they never fumbled. He took the back off each radio and laid out the insides on a clean cloth, like a doctor laying out tools.

"This tube is good," he said, holding up a small glass bottle with wires inside. "From the first one. This coil is good. From the second. And this speaker is perfect. From the third."

"So none of them works," said Teyo.

"No," said Brann. "But together they make one that does."

He heated the soldering iron in the coal brazier and handed it to Teyo.

Teyo's hand shook. The first joint came out lumpy and gray. He went red and waited for Brann to shout. People usually shouted.

Brann only put his own hand over Teyo's, steadying it. "Slow," he said. "Metal remembers hurry."

The second joint was better. The third was shiny and clean.

When the last wire was in place, Brann turned the knob. Nothing. Then a hum. Then a crackle. Then, thin and tinny, music from the Radio Hold's evening program.

Teyo laughed out loud. He had not laughed in a while.

Brann sat back and looked at his scarred hands for a long time.

"For twenty years I welded plates for the Rocket Hold," he said. "Big steel plates for big rockets. They paid me in cards for the company store, and the rockets went up, and some came down in pieces. I helped make a lot of things that broke." He tapped the little radio. "Fixing is better."

He pushed the radio across the bench to Teyo.

"I can't pay for this," said Teyo.

"You already did," said Brann. "You learned it. Now go and fix someone else's."

That night, on his roof, Teyo tuned past the music into the static, wondering what else was out there to hear.

> **Wisdom:** "You can always change things for the better." — Swappy

Talk about it:

1. Why did Brann say "Fixing is better"? What had he done before?
2. Have you ever repaired something instead of throwing it away? How did it feel?

### Sela's Jar

_Ages 10–12 · Shacados, the Last Woods between the slag heaps · Sela, a valley child named Pell (proposal)_

From the top of the ridge, Shacados looked the way it always did: black slag heaps, chimneys, a brown sky. Then the path dipped through a gap in the rocks, and Pell saw green.

She had heard about the Last Woods, but she had never believed in them. A whole valley of trees, alive. Birds. A spring of water so clear you could see the stones at the bottom.

"Shh," said Sela, the seed keeper. "We do not tell the Wardens about this place. We do not tell anyone who would cut it down. Can you keep a secret?"

Pell nodded.

Sela led her between the trees. Some of the leaves had yellow, crinkled edges. "Acid rain," said Sela, touching one. "Every year a little more. But every year we plant a little more too."

Under the roots of the oldest tree there was a door. Behind it, in a cool dark cellar, were shelves and shelves of glass jars. Each jar had a handwritten label: oak, pine, wheat, apple, bean.

"These are seeds," said Sela. "Some are older than my grandmother. We keep them for after."

"After what?" asked Pell.

"After the smoke," said Sela. "One day, the air will be clean again. When that day comes, someone will need these."

Pell frowned. "But that's so far away. You might not even see it."

"Probably not," said Sela simply. She picked one jar off the shelf and shook a single seed into Pell's palm. It was small and brown and hard. "That's why it matters."

They walked to the edge of the woods, where the green stopped and the black slag began. Sela had dug a hole there and filled it with compost and crushed charcoal. Pell pressed the seed into the soil with her thumb.

"Water it," said Sela, and handed her a tin cup from the spring.

Pell poured. The water soaked into the dark earth and disappeared.

"Now what?"

"Now you come back next week and water it again," said Sela. "And the week after. And one day, when you are old, a child you will never meet will sit in its shade and not even know your name."

Pell looked at the patch of wet soil, and then at the black slag, and then at the green valley behind her. She thought it was the saddest and the most hopeful thing she had ever heard.

She came back the next week.

> **Wisdom:** "Plant for someone you will never meet." — Sela

Talk about it:

1. Why does Sela plant trees she may never see grow up?
2. What could you do today that would help someone in the future?

## For teens and adults (13+)

### The Wall

_Ages 13+ · Raya, a glacier valley in early spring · Sora (proposal), Professor Acorn_

Like every Rayan adult, Sora could switch off her own Guard whenever she chose. At nineteen, she never had. She had waited for the right wall.

It stood above the glacier, three hundred meters of gray granite with the morning sun just reaching its top. No tower stood close enough to catch a falling climber. No flight suit. Just rock, chalk and her own hands.

"Guard off," her Orbi said quietly. "Only for you. Everyone else is still safe from you, and you from them."

"I know," said Sora.

Professor Acorn had walked up with her that morning, slowly, leaning on a stick. He sat down on a boulder by the ice, poured tea from a flask, and did not say a word about danger. She had expected him to. Everyone else had.

"You're not going to tell me not to?" she asked.

"It is your body and your choice," he said. "I came because I wanted to watch something brave. And because if it goes badly, someone should be here."

She did not like that he said it out loud. She liked even less that it was true. Renewal could mend broken bones in days. It could not mend everything.

She climbed.

The first hundred meters were a conversation between her fingers and the stone. The second hundred were harder. At two hundred and forty meters, a hold she trusted broke off in her hand. For one long second she hung by her left arm alone, her feet kicking at nothing, the glacier very far below. Her heart beat so hard she could hear it.

No arm stopped her fall, because there was no fall. There was only her, finding another crack with her right hand, and breathing.

At the top, the sun hit her face. She sat down on the edge, shaking and laughing, and switched her Guard back on. Not because anyone asked. Because the wall was done, and she wanted to go home.

Far below, a tiny figure raised a tiny cup.

That evening they sat by a small stove in the valley.

"Were you afraid?" Acorn asked.

"Yes. Very."

"Good," he said. "Then it was really yours." He sipped his tea. "I was afraid all morning too. That was mine. I did not give it to you. That is the hardest part of loving someone free: you let go, gently, and you stay."

> **Wisdom:** "Let go gently." — Letgo

Talk about it:

1. On Raya, every adult may switch off their own Guard, but nobody else's. Is that fair? Where should freedom stop?
2. Professor Acorn was afraid but did not stop Sora. When is it right to step back and let someone take their own risk?

### The Word Kesh Said

_Ages 13+ · Shacados, the launch pad of the Rocket Hold · Kesh, Holder Vask_

The booster stood on its pusher plate like a cathedral built by people who had never been allowed inside one. Riveted steel, weld seams, stains running down from every joint. When it launched, a chain of bombs would explode under the plate one after another and push it into the sky. The wind would carry the dust east, over the shanty towns, as it always did.

The probe on top would go on alone, steered by relays and a clockwork timer. But the relays failed in the shaking of the climb, so someone had to ride a capsule on the booster's nose and fire the bombs by hand, all the way up to orbit.

Kesh had flown that seat once before. He was the only one who had come back.

At the crew gate, on the morning of the launch, he put his helmet down on a crate.

The Warden looked at it. "Pick it up," he said. He did not sound angry. He sounded tired, and a little afraid.

"No," said Kesh.

"Your family's debt —"

"I know about the debt." Kesh's voice was quiet. "Three of my crew went up last time to pay theirs. I watched their capsule come apart. I will fly when the rockets are safe. Not before."

The Warden wrote his name in a book. Everybody knew what the book meant: no card, no bread. Then the Warden did something he did not have to do. He took a ration bar from his own pocket and put it on the crate next to the helmet, and walked away.

At noon the booster launched anyway. Kesh watched the flashes climb the sky from the barracks yard, with a scarf over his face. The young pilot who took his seat fired the bombs by hand up to orbit and came down that afternoon on a parachute, coughing, alive. The probe went on alone, toward the brightest star.

That evening a car stopped at the barracks. It was clean, which nothing in the warm belt ever was. Holder Vask got out alone.

He was younger than Kesh had expected, and thinner.

"They told me a pilot refused," said Vask. "I wanted to see who."

"Here I am," said Kesh.

Vask was quiet for a while. "My father says fear keeps people working. He says if one man says no, a hundred will follow." He looked at the empty pad, at the smoke still hanging in the east, red in the sunset. "I have read the failure reports. All of them. I think you are right about the rockets."

"Then stop the launches."

"I can't. Not yet. I don't have the votes in my own family." Vask's hands were shaking slightly. "But I can delay the next one. I can ask for the welders' reports. I can ask why the fallout always goes east."

"Asking isn't stopping."

"No," said Vask. "But it's how stopping starts."

Two weeks later, the next launch was postponed, for reasons nobody would explain.

One small word had become a slightly bigger one.

> **Wisdom:** "Small words build big ideas." — Prime Sprites

Talk about it:

1. Kesh, the Warden and Vask each did one small thing. Which one mattered most, and why?
2. Vask says, "Asking isn't stopping, but it's how stopping starts." Do you agree? Can you think of a real example from history?

### Thirteen, Seventeen

_Ages 13+ · Shacados, the radio roof, and a Rayan frontier station · Teyo, Wren, Old Brann, a Rayan station keeper_

Teyo had written the numbers on his palm so many times that the ink would not wash off: 2, 3, 5, 7, 11.

"Primes," Wren had said in the night-school cellar, her eyes very bright in the lamplight. "Numbers that can only be divided by one and themselves. A storm cannot make them. A machine that broke cannot make them. Only someone who counts."

"Someone where?" Teyo had asked.

Wren had pointed up, through the ceiling, through the smoke, at the sky.

Now the three of them sat on Teyo's tin roof under one blanket: Teyo, Wren, and Old Brann, who had built the transmitter from leftovers and muttered about what the Radio Hold would do if it found out. The night was cold and rare and clear. Above the gas flares, the stars were out.

"Go on," said Brann.

Teyo put his finger on the brass key and tapped thirteen pulses into the dark.

Then they waited.

"Light is fast," said Wren, mostly to keep everyone calm, "but the sky is big. It could take hours. Days."

They drank weak tea and waited until the stars moved behind the chimneys. Nothing came. Teyo went to bed sure he had imagined everything.

The next night, the answer came: seventeen pulses.

Teyo laughed so loud that Brann had to cover his mouth, though Brann was laughing too.

Then nothing came, night after night. Teyo climbed up anyway. On the twenty-fourth night, a picture came, line by line, beeps and gaps that Wren drew as dots on her slate. A large circle. A small circle. A line between them.

---

Far away, eleven and a half days after Teyo tapped his thirteen pulses, on a station of white ceramic rings around a dark asteroid, a young woman sat in a quiet garden, among potted plants, next to a scorched steel probe. She had touched its weld marks many times. Someone had built it by hand, from very little, and had sent it out to take.

Her Orbi blinked. "A reply," it said. "Thirteen. Our probe out there answered seventeen, as planned."

She closed her eyes. The station's systems had decided long ago how to answer, in a thousandth of a second. But she had asked to send the next one herself.

"The picture," she said. "Two worlds. One line."

"Sent," said the Orbi. "Eleven and a half days to our probe, and a few hours more to them."

"I know," she said. "I'll wait."

On both worlds, two people who would never stand on the same ground looked up at the same strip of sky, each toward the other's bright star, and waited for the next number.

> **Wisdom:** "There is always one more way to link." — Linky

Talk about it:

1. Why did Wren and the station keeper both choose prime numbers? What makes math a good first language between strangers?
2. Shacados sent a probe to take; Raya answered with a message. What would you send to a world you knew almost nothing about?

## Writing new stories

New stories are welcome. Every story should pass the [adorability gate](adorability-gate.md) for its tier and keep the [tone of voice](README.md#tone-of-voice) of the lore core.

1. **Short and warm.** Use short sentences and everyday words, and find humor in surprise, sound and clumsy bodies. Never aim sarcasm at the reader. For ages 1–3, repeat one pattern ("Hop, hop, hop.") so a child can join in.
2. **Problems are puzzles.** Trouble is a puzzle, a misunderstanding or a lost thing, never violence. Nobody loses. Mistakes are learning ("Oops! Let's try again!"), and no story ends in a "Game Over".
3. **Discoveries, not lectures.** Let the characters find the lesson by looking, asking and trying. The wisdom line comes at the end, once, and the story has already shown it.
4. **Gentle feelings, no fear.** Sadness may be gentle, and Nully is never scary ("nap", not "void"). Use no jump scares and no fear-based urgency. Death does not appear below 13+. Shacados appears only from 10+, and only through sharing, repair and hope. In 13+ stories, hard things are named honestly but stay off the page, and someone always shares, mends, teaches or refuses to harm.
5. **Stay inside the canon.** Raya follows the [rules of the home world](raya.md): ask first, copy instead of take, a copy takes one night, Chord flight only near towers and only in a flight suit, and an Orbi never pretends to be a person. Shacados has no technology beyond Earth's 1960s. New names and facts are marked _(proposal)_.
6. **Two layers.** Write for the child and for the adult reading aloud, Pixar-style. A small second layer (a pun, a piece of real science, a quiet question about freedom or fairness) gives the grown-up something to enjoy and talk about. End with one wisdom line and two "Talk about it" questions.
