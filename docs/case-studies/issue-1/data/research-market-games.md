## 3. Legal principles and cases

### 3.1 Core principle: ideas and rules are free, expression is not

- US Copyright Office: copyright does not protect "the idea for a game, its name or title, or the method or methods for playing it". Rule text, artwork and board design can be protected. https://www.copyright.gov/register/tx-games.html ; archived FL-108: https://webharvest.gov/peth04/20041015023438/http://www.copyright.gov/fls/fl108.html
- Statutory basis: 17 U.S.C. 102(b) excludes any "idea, procedure, process, system, method of operation". https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/17/102
- Scènes à faire: elements that naturally follow from a genre or theme are not protectable. In Data East v. Epyx, 862 F.2d 204 (9th Cir. 1988), the court reversed an infringement finding for a karate game because the similarities came from karate itself. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Data_East_USA,_Inc._v._Epyx,_Inc.

### 3.5 Practical clean-room checklist for each game

1. Choose mechanics that are old, documented in public sources, and implemented by many unrelated parties.
2. Use original names. Search the USPTO, EUIPO (TMview) and WIPO Global Brand Database before naming. https://www.tmdn.org/tmview/ ; https://branddb.wipo.int/
3. Use original art, characters, sound, UI layout and colour palette. Do not imitate a famous trade dress (Tetris, Wordle, Candy Crush).
4. Generate or hand-design your own levels and puzzles. Never copy level files or word lists.
5. Use only permissively licensed dependencies (MIT, Apache-2.0, BSD, zlib). Keep a licence inventory, especially if the games themselves are open source.
6. For store listings, do not use competitor names as keywords, since Apple and Google reject keyword stuffing.

## 4. Educational design evidence, kid-safe policies and dark patterns

### 4.1 Learning-science evidence

| Finding | Value | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Spacing effect (distributed practice beats massed practice) | Meta-analysis of 839 assessments in 317 experiments. Cepeda et al. 2006, Psych Bull 132(3):354-80. | https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16719566/ |

| Streaks and habit | Duolingo: learners with a 7-day streak are 3.6x more likely to complete a course | https://blog.duolingo.com/how-duolingo-streak-builds-habit |

| Donor-funded, ad-free model | Khan Academy Kids: ages 2 to 8, free, no ads, no subscriptions, funded by philanthropy | https://learn.khanacademy.org/khan-academy-kids/ |

Design implications:
- Use stealth learning. The fact is part of the mechanic, such as choosing the correct answer gate or merging letters into a word, not a quiz that interrupts play.
- Use spaced repetition: resurface missed items after 1, 3 and 7 days. Use retrieval practice (recall rather than re-read) and immediate, gentle feedback.
- Use a mastery path: start very easy and adapt difficulty so the player gets about 70 to 85% correct (heuristic, unverified as a single number).
- Make streaks kind: free streak freezes, "weekend off", and no loss messaging aimed at children.
- Measure learning, not only retention: run in-game pre and post micro-checks and publish them openly.

### 4.4 Dark patterns to avoid (policy for all our games)

| Dark pattern | Why to avoid | Our alternative |
|---|---|---|
| Paid random rewards (loot boxes, gacha) | Gambling law risk (BE); store policies; harmful to kids | Deterministic shop: you see exactly what you buy |
| Confusing purchase flows or accidental purchases | Grounds for the FTC v. Epic $245M refunds | Clear prices in real currency, a confirmation step, a parental gate, and easy refunds |
| Premium currency obfuscation (gems hiding the real price) | Misleading, especially to kids | Show prices in local currency; if a soft currency exists, make it earnable only |
| Guilt or FOMO pressure ("your pet is sad and will die") | Manipulative toward children | The pet waits happily and nothing is lost while away |
| Streak loss as punishment | Anxiety | Free streak freezes, celebrate the return |
| Forced or interstitial ads, ads disguised as gameplay | Kid-safety and store-policy risk | No ads at launch. Later, rewarded ads only, opt-in, and never in kids mode. |
| Pay-to-win or energy systems that block learning | Blocks the educational purpose | Learning content is never paywalled. Paid items are cosmetic or convenience only. |
| Social pressure (public leaderboards for kids, open chat) | COPPA and FTC chat-by-default ruling | No open chat for kids, emoji-only reactions, private family leaderboards |
| Hard-to-cancel subscriptions | FTC "click to cancel" scrutiny; store rules | One-tap cancel, reminder before renewal |
| Collecting unnecessary data | COPPA 2025 data minimization | No accounts needed; local-first saves; optional parent account |

### 5.6 Community building (practical)

### 6.1 Patterns in cute IP that scales

1. **Simple, recognizable silhouette.** Readable as a 16px icon, a sticker or a plush (Hello Kitty, Pusheen, Om Nom, Molang). Low detail, flat colours, two or three main colours.
2. **Character-first, game-agnostic.** Om Nom and Talking Tom moved across games, short video, series and toys. One mascot or a small cast should appear in every mini game, so each game markets the character and the character markets every game.
3. **Expressive but minimal faces.** Big eyes, small or no mouth, emotion carried by body pose. Works across cultures.
4. **Short-form animation and stickers first.** Pusheen's 30B GIF shares and Om Nom's YouTube views were cheap reach before merch.
5. **Licensing comes after audience.** Licensees follow proven reach. Merch revenue in Pokémon and Sanrio dwarfs game revenue, but only after years of audience building.
6. **Own the IP outright.** Register trademarks for character names in the key classes early: 9 (software), 16 (stationery), 25 (apparel), 28 (toys) and 41 (entertainment). Keep the art under a clear licence. Code can be open source (MIT/GPL) while character art and trademarks stay protected, as Mindustry and many open-source games do with assets and names. Alternatively, licence art under CC-BY-NC with trademarks reserved (unverified as best practice; consult counsel).

### 6.2 Notes on chibi style

- Chibi (Japanese "small person", also "super deformed" or SD) uses head-to-body ratios of about 1:1 to 1:3, oversized heads and eyes, and simplified limbs. It lowers art cost per pose and animates well at small sizes, which suits mobile screens. (Common art-practice knowledge; no single authoritative source, so the specific ratios are unverified.)
- Baby-schema ("Kindchenschema", Lorenz 1943) features are a large head, big low-set eyes and round shapes. Studies show these features increase perceived cuteness and caretaking motivation in adults (Glocker et al. 2009, Ethology 115(3):257-263). https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1439-0310.2008.01603.x
- This makes chibi a good fit across toddlers, children and adults (the kawaii appeal of Sanrio and Pusheen to adults is demonstrated by the sales above).
- Chibi is a style, not property: anyone can use it. Only specific characters are protected, so design an original cast and avoid resemblance to existing Sanrio, Pokémon or Line Friends characters.

### 7.1 Distribution and monetization sequence (suggested, derived from the data above)

1. **Launch.** Web (HTML5, for portals like Poki and CrazyGames and our own site), Telegram Mini App and a PWA. All free, no ads, no accounts, local saves. Donations open at launch: GitHub Sponsors (0% from personal accounts), Open Collective and Ko-fi.
2. **Store apps.** Kids-safe builds (Apple Kids Category, Google Families) with no ads and parental gates.
3. **Supporter subscription.** Cosmetic only, with all learning free. Merch only after the mascot has an audience (stickers and digital packs first, then plush through a licensed manufacturer).
4. **Optional rewarded ads.** Adult or general-audience mode only, opt-in, with a generous reward. Never in kids mode. The eCPM benchmark is about $16 to $20 per 1,000 views in tier-1 markets.
5. **Never.** Loot boxes, paid random rewards, crypto tokens, interstitials for kids, open chat for kids, or paywalled learning.
